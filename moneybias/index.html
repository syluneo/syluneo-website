          The credit card trap 💳

          Paying only the "minimum amount due" keeps your card in good standing, but the unpaid part keeps collecting interest. See what that could mean month by month.

          How your first two payments work

          Statement 1 is your bill as entered, so it has no interest yet. From statement 2, whatever was left collects interest and GST before your next payment. Rounded to the rupee; the month-by-month schedule shows paise.

          What you'd owe on each statement

          Minimum due onlyYour monthly amount
          See it month by month

          Why the minimum due can get expensive

          • Interest builds on what's left. Each month, the unpaid part collects interest at your monthly rate, plus 18% GST on that interest. The next minimum is worked out on the new, bigger total.
          • The minimum is a small slice. When most of a payment goes to interest and GST, very little reduces the debt, so clearing it can take years.
          • Paying more shortens it a lot. Every rupee above the interest goes straight to the debt, which shrinks next month's interest too.
          • Paying in full avoids purchase interest. Paying the full statement balance by the due date generally means no interest on purchases, subject to your card's terms.
          • An unpaid balance can cost you the interest-free period. RBI rules allow it to be suspended if any part of last month's bill is unpaid, so new purchases may collect interest straight away.
          • Check your own card. Every issuer sets its own rates and minimum-due rules. They're in your card's Most Important Terms and Conditions (MITC).

          Thinking of converting a purchase to EMI? Check the no-cost EMI calculator →

          The lifestyle inflation trap 📈

          A raise can quietly disappear if spending rises just as fast. See how much of yours could stick.

          Before and after the raise

          ExpensesSurplus (income − expenses)ShortfallIncome line
          Raise absorbed by spending–
          Change in monthly surplus–

          Three ideas that could help

          1. When income rises, raise your automatic saving first, for example by half the raise, before upgrading anything.
          2. Wait a month or two before taking on new recurring costs such as rent, subscriptions or EMIs. They're harder to undo than one-off buys.
          3. Set a savings target for the new income before deciding how much extra to spend.

          Spending more isn't always a mistake. Rent, family responsibilities, transport and living costs can genuinely rise with a new job or city. The aim is to choose the change, not drift into it.

          MoneyBias: How privacy works

          Privacy

          How privacy works 🔒

          • No account, no sign-up. There is no login and nothing to register.
          • Your calculations happen in your browser. Quiz answers and every number you type into a tool are worked out on your device. The page has no code that sends them anywhere: no server, database, analytics, cookies or tracking.
          • Nothing is stored. The page doesn't save your inputs in browser storage, and money fields ask your browser not to autofill or remember them. Closing or reloading the page clears everything.
          • Fonts are part of the site. No outside font or image services are contacted.
          • Sharing is your choice. Share images and messages are made on your device and only leave it if you download, copy or share them. Tool images leave out your numbers unless you tick the box.
          • Links you share contain no personal data. A challenge or Face-Off link only names a personality type.

          Like any website, the host that serves the page (GitHub Pages for syluneo.com) may keep standard server logs of page requests, such as IP address and browser type. Those logs never include what you type, because your inputs are never sent.

          The biases below are well-established findings, studied in many experiments. MoneyBias itself is not a scientific test.
